you know that feeling when you check three unrelated tabs and realize they're actually the same story? that happened to me this week. one tab was Kenya's latest telecom stats, one was a Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) supervision report, and one was a bunch of American church leaders arguing with Microsoft. turns out they're all about the same thing: who builds the internet's next layer of infrastructure, who pays for it, and who gets left holding the bill.

## fixed internet in Kenya just had a real year

fixed line phones are basically dead in Kenya. domestic fixed voice traffic collapsed 89.4% year on year, and the country is down to 6,085 fixed line subscriptions total, according to the [Communications Authority's Q4 2025/2026 sector report as covered by Techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/18/fixed-line-internet-connections-kenya/). nobody is shocked by that. what's more interesting is what replaced it: fixed data and internet subscriptions grew 32.4% to 2.84 million connections, driven mostly by fiber (up 29.7% to 1.57 million subscriptions) and, surprisingly, satellite (up 54.4%).

Starlink already holds a 1% market share with 27,616 subscriptions, ahead of some long-established local ISPs. the same report notes that satellite bandwidth capacity actually fell 19.1% even as subscriptions rose, because low earth orbit systems like Starlink need far less bandwidth allocation per household than the old VSAT technology they're replacing. if you're building anything that assumes "the user is on 3G," it's worth updating that assumption. Safaricom still leads fixed data with 36.1% market share, but the tail is getting more crowded, and DSL is down to 23 subscriptions nationwide (rest in peace).

## the banks are becoming API companies, whether they meant to or not

CBK's 2025 Bank Supervision Annual Report tells a similar infrastructure story from a different angle. 84% of commercial banks and 79% of microfinance banks have now adopted APIs, making it the single most common piece of new banking technology, per [Techweez's breakdown of the report](https://techweez.com/2026/09/22/cbk-banking-fintech-report-2025/). half of all surveyed financial institutions have adopted some form of AI, mostly for credit risk assessment (65%), cybersecurity (54%), and customer service (43%). only 30% have a formal AI strategy though, and 93% of respondents want CBK to issue actual AI governance rules. that gap between "we deployed it" and "we have a policy for it" is exactly where incidents happen.

it's not a coincidence that [WSO2Con Africa 2026](https://techweez.com/2026/09/25/wso2con-africa-2026-nairobi/) picked Nairobi for its first African edition, running under the theme "Building the Agentic Enterprise" and pulling in Kenyan government ICT officials alongside WSO2's product leadership. APIs, identity, and integration platforms are the plumbing behind everything the CBK report describes. if you work in fintech in East Africa, this is your stack now: APIs, cloud, and whatever governance framework shows up to regulate the AI layer sitting on top of it.

## somebody has to pay for the AI, and that somebody might be you

here's the less charming side of that same infrastructure boom. Techweez [reported on "LLM-jacking,"](https://techweez.com/2026/09/28/llm-jacking-cloud-ai-cybercrime/) a cybercrime trend Google has flagged where attackers steal cloud credentials or API keys (leaked GitHub tokens, session tokens lifted by malware) and quietly spin up GPU-heavy virtual machines to run their own AI models on your bill. Google's Mandiant team investigated at least one case where an exposed personal access token was used to deploy unauthorized AI infrastructure at scale, leaving the customer to cover the compute costs. the fix is unglamorous: rotate leaked keys immediately, prefer short-lived credentials with least-privilege scopes, set billing alerts and GPU quota limits, and keep dev, test, and production in separate accounts so one stolen key can't wander everywhere.

Microsoft's own threat intelligence team separately flagged "Storm-3168," a cluster of agentic-driven cloud attacks that abuse compromised service principals, per the [Microsoft security writeup](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMizwFBVV95cUxNM092d3lCWFRTMmMxZ09DODM4Z3hxWUNzSV93OVFoYUU3T2tYTUxudnMyc2MzR1R0QU1QSEI2ZzJQRjNfaUNVOUVRUU9LLTNSalE4SkEyM0FWcHN0MDlJb2dwcU8zNUhtbDYySjJLU3VYaVBvb1lLQUI2T2huNTduTkRiQjRYcWJ4WG9HNGpZSFNrbW5iSDVLSVdwUXpmRUdQTWt4RHVfUm5RQ1NJaDBKYWZjMDdNY0RGREpSeWZUeVlsVmd0eVJXZS1PM2xqWkU?oc=5) circulating via Google News. service principals are the non-human identities apps use to talk to cloud APIs, and they don't get MFA prompts or suspicious-login emails when someone steals them, which is exactly why they're a favorite target right now. nvidia has apparently responded to the same pressure by shipping a software platform meant to stop AI agents from misbehaving, according to a [CNBC Africa report picked up via Google News](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiogFBVV95cUxQRWhmVDJGODhTOUJGeFdXajVQNHVRSEdfMUM3bWtmS1FUUXlUdzFpR2dRbFVlTUtDMDRpbEhaVExFRDZRSHhLaFFkRWFhYjAwZUxrYm9qVTFyc1B2R25MTWZlRThsRGpZbE90amlOUFpERzJQR05UeGtoVlppb1lrUUltdkl2ZDZYN0otazdVZ29rcVJGMU55OVVfc2FyRjZtRlE?oc=5). read that as a signal, not a review: agent guardrails are becoming a product category, not a nice-to-have.

## who actually pays for the data center next door

zoom out further and the same "who pays" question shows up at the physical layer. in Indiana, a coalition of church groups called We Make Indiana is publicly asking Microsoft for a "fair share" agreement, essentially 1% of a planned data center's costs, to offset the strain on local electric bills and infrastructure, and Microsoft has reportedly gone quiet on the request, according to [Ars Technica's reporting](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/09/microsoft-goes-quiet-after-church-groups-ask-for-1-of-data-center-costs/). the group already blocked one rumored Meta data center in the same area and says it isn't trying to stop Microsoft's project (construction starts this fall, expected operational by 2029), just make sure the deal is proportional.

that fight isn't unique to Indiana. Rest of World reports that [South Africa is joining a wider pattern of resistance against American-built data centers](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMidEFVX3lxTE1pUEN0VEhLQ2Z4MWdudzR0SnBpMVRhazFCOTJ3Y1V6VVctOUV5aXdqRjhGWmtkcnRyYTVWUkdEeG8wTEtqY2p4V3RnQzRGSlhSV2xrRFVUQTYwRDZYMlRqZW1nYV9TOXVJczNTbmNMbXhxSGZf?oc=5), while Forbes has floated the opposite framing: that the same data centers facing backlash in the US could bring power infrastructure benefits to rural Africa, per a [headline circulating via Google News](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMitAFBVV95cUxNVDRldUVsbUtXUko2ZE95SVN4emdMWVIyTl82WjNmeGt2NFZmWUpCLWVvdEl6cnRtMTVrV1RzSno2S1d5TUdYRUdoVnIxVmlGMV9uY0RpZ1lZTFo1N2hSMHR1WVF2MmtQVHJ5U2NZcTZfVGxaOXpMR2lkb0p1NG1hWTF2OTV0ZldSSkpDX094TVg2T3JWLUFzLUV4ZEo2VVJ0QVNRWDN4U2lDZ3RYd25TeXBLVk0?oc=5). i'd flag that as an unverified claim rather than settled fact, since the underlying article wasn't available to check. either way, the pattern here matches Indiana and Kenya's own fiber rollout: infrastructure decisions used to be purely technical, and now they're community decisions too.
