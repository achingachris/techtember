## fiber finally beats the fixed line

Kenya's fixed internet subscriptions grew 32% to 2.84 million connections, and the old copper fixed-line phone is basically dead. The growth is coming from fiber, satellite (Starlink included), and wireless broadband, not from traditional telephony ([techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/18/fixed-line-internet-connections-kenya/)). if you build anything that assumes a customer is on 3G or a shared cyber cafe connection, that assumption is aging fast. Fiber-to-the-home changes what you can ship: bigger web apps, video-heavy onboarding, real-time dashboards, without worrying about every megabyte.

This matters for anyone doing React or Next.js work targeting Kenyan users. You can stop over-optimizing for 2G fallback paths as the default case, though you should still test for them, because coverage is uneven between Nairobi and everywhere else.

## banks are becoming fintechs, whether they meant to or not

The Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) published a report showing that Kenyan banks are adopting artificial intelligence (AI), application programming interfaces (APIs), cloud infrastructure, and digital lending at a pace that basically makes them fintechs with a banking license ([techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/22/cbk-banking-fintech-report-2025/)). Pair that with a new payment bill that would force banks and M-PESA to open up their data to third parties ([techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/28/llm-jacking-cloud-ai-cybercrime/)), and you get a market that is inching toward open banking whether the incumbents are ready or not.

If you're building fintech products in Kenya, this is your cue to get comfortable with API-first architecture now. Banks that resisted integration for a decade are suddenly the ones asking vendors for OAuth flows and webhook support. WSO2 held its first WSO2Con Africa in Nairobi around the same theme: enterprise leaders discussing how APIs and AI fit into African infrastructure ([techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/25/wso2con-africa-2026-nairobi/)). Django's REST framework and DRF-based API gateways are about to get a lot more real-world traffic.

## the AI agent security mess nobody priced in

Here's the part that should worry you more than it probably does: Google is warning that attackers are hijacking cloud accounts specifically to run AI models on someone else's bill, a practice reported as "LLM-jacking" ([techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/28/llm-jacking-cloud-ai-cybercrime/)). Stolen credentials, compromised service principals, and idle cloud quotas are turned into free GPU time for the attacker while you get the invoice.

Microsoft separately disclosed a threat actor tracked as Storm-3168 running agentic-driven cloud attacks through compromised service principals, meaning the attacker isn't just stealing a password, they're hijacking the automated identity your AI agent or pipeline uses to act on your behalf ([Microsoft via Google News](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMizwFBVV95cUxNM092d3lCWFRTMmMxZ09DODM4Z3hxWUNzSV93OVFoYUU3T2tYTUxudnMyc2MzR1R0QU1QSEI2ZzJQRjNfaUNVOUVRUU9LLTNSalE4SkEyM0FWcHN0MDlJb2dwcU8zNUhtbDYySjJLU3VYaVBvb1lLQUI2T2huNTduTkRiQjRYcWJ4WG9HNGpZSFNrbW5iSDVLSVdwUXpmRUdQTWt4RHVfUm5RQ1NJaDBKYWZjMDdNY0RGREpSeWZUeVlsVmd0eVJXZS1PM2xqWkU?oc=5)). If your service principal has broad permissions "just in case," this is exactly the scenario that bites you.

Nvidia's response is a new software platform aimed at stopping AI agents from misbehaving, essentially guardrails and policy enforcement for agentic systems that act with real permissions ([CNBC Africa via Google News](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiogFBVV95cUxQRWhmVDJGODhTOUJGeFdXajVQNHVRSEdfMUM3bWtmS1FUUXlUdzFpR2dRbFVlTUtDMDRpbEhaVExFRDZRSHhLaFFkRWFhYjAwZUxrYm9qVTFyc1B2R25MTWZlRThsRGpZbE90amlOUFpERzJQR05UeGtoVlppb1lrUUltdkl2ZDZYN0otazdVZ29rcVJGMU55OVVfc2FyRjZtRlE?oc=5), also described separately as a platform for managing AI agent security ([Engineering.com via Google News](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMijwFBVV95cUxOZTBQTG85RTJ5X1hUYmJUWWVBLVZHLVJ6SmxDd3dmT3ZFc3dVXzV6a0NfSTJEZmNxbTJUQWJXLXBIdzNTWnhZcEpZYlBBWFJ3Z2NPd193Z2xzQjRya042cmhKQjc4UDF6QTFrbVhZTUdnT1A2NzViNE90aDZ4WUZ3MC1YWk5nbi16OEZDN2JMQQ?oc=5)). Nvidia is also positioning itself as an infrastructure layer for AI agents through a new alliance with Google aimed at speeding up data center connections ([Mexico Business News via Google News](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiqAFBVV95cUxPVVdWYjd0M0g2QnRiTTBObGN5dW5kTnhQakNvem1iQzBGajE1VlJySGhsdGl0a1JiR2k3UTd3VnJ3ZGhnTHIxak8zMGhTbzk2S3JFV2pKU2J5Wjhic295UGJCN25Fdml3NThaU3hNRFhlbWxaSEdzYnkyd1M0SC1URFdFUG8yRTRWS2hpVGgtYnQ1RFFNM0lWdHdIMFY5Nk5FbXFwRFJaQWs?oc=5). The pattern across all three items is the same: the industry is racing to give agents real-world permissions faster than it's building the fences around them.

For your own projects, the practical takeaway is boring but important: rotate credentials for any agent or pipeline with cloud spend access, scope service principals to the narrowest permission set they need, and set hard budget alerts on AI or GPU spend. Don't wait for a surprise invoice to find out your API key had more reach than you thought.

## data centers, backlash, and where the power actually goes

The physical infrastructure behind all this AI compute is generating friction. Church groups in the US have asked Microsoft to commit 1% of data center costs to their communities, and Microsoft has gone quiet in response ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/09/microsoft-goes-quiet-after-church-groups-ask-for-1-of-data-center-costs/)). South Africa is reportedly joining a broader global pushback against American-owned data centers being built locally ([Rest of World via Google News](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMidEFVX3lxTE1pUEN0VEhLQ2Z4MWdudzR0SnBpMVRhazFCOTJ3Y1V6VVctOUV5aXdqRjhGWmtkcnRyYTVWUkdEeG8wTEtqY2p4V3RnQzRGSlhSV2xrRFVUQTYwRDZYMlRqZW1nYV9TOXVJczNTbmNMbXhxSGZf?oc=5). At the same time, a Forbes piece argues that the same data center model resented in the US could bring reliable power to rural Africa if structured differently ([forbes.com via Google News](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMitAFBVV95cUxNVDRldUVsbUtXUko2ZE95SVN4emdMWVIyTl82WjNmeGt2NFZmWUpCLWVvdEl6cnRtMTVrV1RzSno2S1d5TUdYRUdoVnIxVmlGMV9uY0RpZ1lZTFo1N2hSMHR1WVF2MmtQVHJ5U2NZcTZfVGxaOXpMR2lkb0p1NG1hWTF2OTV0ZldSSkpDX094TVg2T3JWLUFzLUV4ZEo2VVJ0QVNRWDN4U2lDZ3RYd25TeXBLVk0?oc=5).

This is a genuine disagreement worth naming rather than smoothing over: the same infrastructure is framed as an unwanted burden in one region and a potential power lifeline in another. Which framing wins probably depends less on the technology and more on who owns the electricity contract and who benefits from the jobs. As a Kenyan engineer, this is worth watching closely given AWS already has regional data zone footprint disruptions (Amazon's UAE cloud data zone in Bahrain remains unrestored after war damage, per Reuters, showing how fragile these "always up" cloud regions really are ([Reuters via Google News](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMizAFBVV95cUxPcGhFMHZVNGthdVl1bjYza3ZOWVpRY3ZMS1JmZkgxVnJTWGRhZFdFS3JfYk1nX2Y1Nzl3RlZDN21lWFA3b0xUU1E3UkdZR0hPQXNXdG5seEhsQ0hUR1gxVnZCWHFpTXdQdGhGQkFKTFd0cW80QUNBdnpXYzdOX25zZEJpN2lkOW1IWVlnc0RZNDk0cGgzSTlNYXFaMS1EcUJTM0ZUUjlhS0FLV0NrbGtsM3BKOVlFOWdFUi1sbGQtWmRqX3ZsODdPczRYRUw?oc=5)).

## the takeaway

None of these stories are really about AI as a headline feature. They're about infrastructure ownership: who controls the fiber, who controls the API, who controls the cloud credentials, and who controls the power plant next to the data center. Kenya's connectivity and fintech numbers show the country building its own layer of that stack. The security stories show what happens when nobody locks the door on the layer below it. Build accordingly: scope your permissions tight, watch your cloud bill like a landlord watches rent, and don't assume "cloud region" means "always available."
