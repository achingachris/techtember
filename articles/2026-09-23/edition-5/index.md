## Kenya's digital plumbing is quietly becoming the real story

Everyone loves a flashy AI demo. Fewer people talk about the plumbing underneath it, which is usually where the actual engineering happens. Kenya's mobile money subscriptions hit 54 million accounts by June 2026, up 13.2 percent, with Safaricom's M-Pesa still holding an 88.8 percent market share ([techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/18/mobile-money-subscriptions-reach-54-million/)). If you're building fintech here, you're building against M-Pesa's rails whether you like it or not. A related Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) report shows Kenyan banks are quietly turning into fintechs themselves, adopting application programming interfaces (APIs), cloud infrastructure, AI, and digital lending to compete ([techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/22/cbk-banking-fintech-report-2025/)). Add a new payment bill that wants to force banks and M-Pesa to open up their data ([techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/18/mobile-money-subscriptions-reach-54-million/)), and open banking pressure is clearly coming to Kenya. If you're a backend engineer, start reading up on consent flows and data-sharing standards now, not after the bill passes.

There's a catch, though: Kenya reportedly has 22,000 digital government services, but the backend behind them is still largely manual ([techweez](https://techweez.com/2026/09/18/mobile-money-subscriptions-reach-54-million/)). That's the gap between launching a portal and actually automating a process, and it's one I've seen in more than one government contract.

## takeaway

The thread connecting all of this: capability (AI vision models, agents, foldable hardware) is arriving faster than the plumbing (security models, backend automation, power and water planning) that's supposed to support it. That gap is where engineering jobs live. Build the boring parts well, and the flashy demo on top of it will actually hold up.
